The Search for Self-Respect, chapter by chapter

A front-to-back read of Maltz's 1973 book: for each chapter, what he's saying, how he builds it, what he asks you to do and how it links to the rest. Book content only, with page numbers.

Fifteen chapters (pp. 3–249). Chapters 1–14 each build one part of self-respect; Chapter 15 gives one Goal Builder exercise for each of them, one a week for fourteen weeks.

The big picture

Maltz calls self-respect "the nucleus" of everything he had written — Psycho-Cybernetics, The Magic Power of Self-Image Psychology, Creative Living for Today (p. 4). "There is no more accurate measure of an individual's value than his own degree of self-respect" (p. 4), and it has no necessary link to money: some of his richest, most famous patients thought of themselves as failures (p. 9).

What the book adds is the word active. Ideas and images "are not in themselves always enough" (p. 59), so sincerity is an active power, forgiveness is active forgiveness, tranquillity is active and not "a hammock," and life needs "no retirement clock." His formula, repeated through the book: constructive thinking → constructive imaging → constructive action (pp. 213, 231).

The book's shape:

  • Chapters 1–3 — what self-respect is, and two foundations: understanding and sincerity.
  • Chapters 4–6 — three enemies: uncertainty, resentment, emptiness.
  • Chapters 7–9 — opportunity, active forgiveness, creative mirror watching.
  • Chapters 10–14 — tearing down self-built prison walls, tranquillity, the active life, integrity, survival.
  • Chapter 15 — fourteen Goal Builders, one exercise for each chapter, done one a week for fourteen weeks, each ending with a word written on your mirror.

Maltz likes to spell things out: RESENTMENT, TRANQUILLITY, INTEGRITY, NO SURVIVAL and SURVIVAL are all acrostics, each letter a habit to drop or build (Reference has every one).

Chapter 1 — Your Great Need: Self-Respect (pp. 3–16)

What he's saying. In a world of skyscrapers, jets and computers, people are still searching for self-respect (pp. 3–4). Without it "you are a criminal in hiding, living in a prison you have built around yourself" (p. 5). It is "Self-respect with a capital S," independent of your bank balance, your neighbors' disapproval or your boss's anger (p. 10).

How he builds it.

  • Reaching out with determination, not a tentative "I'll give it a try anyway" (p. 6). On bad days "steak looks like dogmeat" — laugh and move forward (pp. 6–7).
  • People who reached out: a Detroit man in his late fifties, leg in a cast and pajamas under his overcoat, who travelled 25 miles through snow to hear Maltz; a blind young man teaching Psycho-Cybernetics workshops (pp. 7–8).
  • The quiet room. Maltz's father, a clothes designer on the Lower East Side, raised his hand to a worker who handled cloth with greasy fingers, then went to his private office until he was calm and came back to speak quietly. That's where Maltz got his "quiet room in your mind" (pp. 11–13).
  • The self you like. Clothes, promiscuity and status don't give you an identity: "You have to build a sense of self that you like" (p. 14).

How it connects. Shakespeare's "Nothing is good, I see, without respect" becomes the book's theme (p. 16). Goal Builder 1 is self-respect itself.

Chapter 2 — Understanding: The Key to Human Dignity (pp. 17–32)

What he's saying. We have two tasks, says Maltz, quoting Anatole France: more understanding and less misunderstanding (pp. 17–18). Prejudice is misunderstanding used as armor (pp. 18–19). The gang fights of his boyhood, with sticks, bricks and kettle lids for shields, and "usually no one even remembered how it started" (p. 20).

How he builds it.

  • "There are never enough police" — police yourself first (pp. 21–22).
  • Three parts of understanding (p. 22): identify your needs and work toward them; find who you are in relation to others; see the greatness in yourself and in all people — the self-respect that is "your present to yourself from yourself every day of the year."
  • Stories: a Connecticut mother rejected by her married daughter, where both were wrong (pp. 23–25); the elevator operator whose dull job was "an adventure in pleasantness" (p. 25); Jim Dillard of the Toronto Argonauts, who forgot his blunders and got out of a slump (pp. 25–26).
  • NEW DIGNITY — three principles (pp. 28–31): understand that you are as good as the next fellow ("Let the 'Joneses' keep up with you"); understand that your needs are individual; understand that you have a right to a life of contentment — "imperfectly happy, perhaps."

What he asks you to do. Share understanding, see people as individuals not stereotypes, and "think twice before judging" (pp. 31–32).

Chapter 3 — The Active Power of Sincerity (pp. 33–47)

What he's saying. You build self-respect like a skyscraper, foundation first (p. 33). Sincerity isn't naive turning of the other cheek; it's an active force (pp. 34–35). A man near despair wrote him: "The person I used to know is dead. He is alive. He is I" (pp. 35–37).

How he builds it — four keys to sincerity (pp. 38–44):

  1. Focus on the truth about yourself — assets and liabilities. Perfectionism is the chief obstacle: "you have the right to make mistakes sometimes." Leavenworth prisoners forgive themselves; why can't you?
  2. Develop an inquiring mind. In Maltz's New Faces: New Futures game, a playwright, a lawyer and a doctor misjudged people from pre-surgery masks: the "gangster" with a gash was a self-effacing businessman hurt in a car crash (pp. 40–42).
  3. Build your confidence — relive past successes; play golf in the room of your mind, eye on the ball and follow through (pp. 42–43).
  4. See reality. "Sincerity will fill you with strength; insincerity will exhaust you" (p. 43).

How it connects. "You must be your own hero" (p. 46). The little girl who'll go to bed on time "Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday" shows that mañana means never (p. 47) — a theme that returns in Chapters 6, 12 and 14.

Chapter 4 — Winning Out Over Uncertainty (pp. 48–62)

What he's saying. Building the positive while ignoring the negative "is building on quicksand" (p. 49). Uncertainty is universal: the boss, the spouse, the bomb, a loved one in hospital (pp. 50–51).

How he builds it.

  • His mother's birthday. At 3 a.m. on his terrace after her party, she told him she had breast cancer; she died a year later (pp. 51–52).
  • Laugh when you can. At the 1936 Wimbledon semifinal, Fred Perry stopped to read a drifting scrap of newspaper and laughed until 18,000 people laughed, and Don Budge relaxed (pp. 53–54).
  • Don't take it personally. A taxi driver berated himself for a wrong turn through Central Park: "I don't like myself when I make mistakes like that" (pp. 55–56).
  • A winning approach — five steps (pp. 58–60): renounce passivity; renounce neutrality; choose an active approach (counterpunch like a boxer); set goals every day; allow yourself mistakes — "You win out over life's uncertainties, as a rule, only when you are able to accept your own uncertainties."

How it connects. History was never certain — Crusades, Genghis Khan, witches burned (pp. 60–61). Goal Builder 4 (p. 237) asks you to imagine a terrifying scene so your own problems look manageable.

Chapter 5 — Your Struggle to Overcome Resentment (pp. 63–82)

What he's saying. Resentment is part of the failure mechanism and spreads like an infection (pp. 64–65). It's an emotional diet, "an unpalatable goulash of negativism," and "You are your own fireman" (p. 65). The one person resentment certainly destroys is you (p. 66).

How he builds it.

  • The RESENTMENT complex (pp. 66–69): Revenge, Envy, Sulkiness, Elimination from life, Narrow-mindedness, Temper, Mistake-mindedness, Enmity, Negligence, Tension as a habit. "DANGEROUS, KEEP AWAY."
  • Misguided bravery. A boy hit by an iceball thrown by his own gang gloried in the scar; real bravery was the policeman who jumped into the icy East River in his overcoat to save a child (pp. 70–71).
  • Two men afraid of choking — one whose smothering mother gave her life for him, one who woke choking his wife and resented a father who preferred his brother. To the second: "You are more than your father thinks you are. You are what you think you are" (pp. 71–80).
  • The cab driver who lost 90 pounds. 255 pounds, wife gone with his best friend. Maltz: "I dare you to come see me when you are thin." A year later he was 160–170 pounds and remarried (pp. 75–77).
  • Positive approach — four steps (p. 81): rise above the deprivations of your past; set goals every day; forgive others; forgive yourself every day.

What he asks you to do. Find the real "guts" in rising above resentment, not in belligerence (pp. 81–82). Goal Builder 5 has you list your old worries and check how many came true.

Chapter 6 — Your Third Enemy: Emptiness (pp. 83–96)

What he's saying. People fill emptiness with alcohol, drugs, food and celebrities (p. 84). The "haunted house" of his boyhood was just empty: "Insight is our window; inner peace, our furniture; and the excitement of our goals, the fuel for heating" (pp. 84–85). "You create your own feeling of emptiness" (p. 90).

How he builds it.

  • The Texas salesman and his son phoned: "It worked… I forgave myself, I forgave him" (pp. 86–88).
  • Wishing to be someone else — "If I only had a million dollars"; "twenty years younger — what did you do with your youth when you had it?" (p. 90).
  • Five ways to vanquish emptiness (pp. 91–95): stop sleepwalking; resign from Mañana Incorporated ("never get any dividends"); renew your sense of direction; enrich your self-image; reach out to other people.

How it connects. A friend on night patrol was handed two grenades by an unsearched German prisoner: search out your negative forces and take away the grenades (pp. 95–96). That ends the three-enemies section.

Chapter 7 — Developing the Strength to Seize Opportunities (pp. 97–113)

What he's saying. "Opportunity… never knocks… You are opportunity" (p. 97). Three-fourths of 400 eminent people studied were handicapped in youth (pp. 100–101).

How he builds it.

  • The doctor who liked to sleep asked to learn plastic surgery, overslept, then asked if Maltz operated in the afternoon — and shut his own door (pp. 101–102).
  • The scrubwoman's song. For ten years she hummed while mopping. Her husband had died in a car crash with her and her daughter in the car; the daughter was graduating in psychology to teach underprivileged children. Of her new wig: "Does it make me look like a lady?" "You always were" (pp. 103–106).
  • How to move toward opportunity — five steps (pp. 107–111): watch the red light on your mental dashboard and change it to green; live in the present; stop belittling yourself ("If we were all movie stars, there would be no food on our tables"); set constructive goals; stand up to crises.

How it connects. "By surrendering, we must win" — surrender to compassion, courage, your assets (pp. 112–113). Goal Builder 7 uses Green Eggs and Ham.

Chapter 8 — Real Forgiveness: Active Forgiveness (pp. 114–130)

What he's saying. "Each day is D-day" against an invisible enemy (p. 114). Passive forgiveness is a word, an "abracadabra"; "Real forgiveness… is active forgiveness" (pp. 116–117).

How he builds it.

  • Words only: a couple saying "I forgive you" while still pushing each other (pp. 117–118). In action: a Leavenworth prisoner who, moved to Texarkana, started a 12-week Psycho-Cybernetics course for other inmates (pp. 118–119).
  • Active forgiveness — six steps (pp. 119–122): use foresight; seize opportunity ("drown out your frustrations with the flood of your fulfillments"); develop insight; concentrate on compassion; forget yesterday; try to relax. "Forgiveness to relaxation to pleasure to self-respect is a fine success cycle."
  • The Philadelphia cab driver who walked out on his wife twenty years earlier and still sleeps fine: Maltz doesn't approve, but if he could forgive himself, why can't you forgive small blunders? (pp. 122–127)
  • Don't simply turn the other cheek — protect your rights, then see the other fellow's view and forgive him for being human (pp. 128–129).

What he asks you to do. Self-forgiveness is "the great forgiveness" (p. 129). Asking for a raise is your Everest.

Chapter 9 — The Dynamic Potential for Mirror Watchers (pp. 131–145)

What he's saying. Creative mirror watching isn't Narcissus or vanity but "a clarification of who he is… and a sharper refocusing on the person he can become" (pp. 132–133).

How he builds it.

  • The pigeons on his terrace. "The Runt," with a twisted beak, tried to overcome her handicap. "Brownie" balanced for an hour on one foot in a 4-inch crevice facing the wrong way — "But I'm used to this" (pp. 133–139). Which way are you facing?
  • The man of 40 who lost his insurance job and drank, then said in a flash of passion "I don't want to be poor anymore… I want a good life" — "that was one of your moments of success." A year later he'd lost 20 pounds and was in the investment business (pp. 139–143).
  • The boiled frog gets "used to" the heat (p. 144).

What he asks you to do. "Don't be afraid! That face won't bite you… You must be your best friend" (p. 145). Look through failure to success. Chapter 15 uses the mirror in every exercise.

Chapter 10 — Tear Down Those Prison Walls (pp. 146–161)

What he's saying. Millions are "stool pigeons" who inform on themselves (pp. 147–148). Walls go up after a shock — a death, a lost job, rejection — and "We must get back to ourselves" (pp. 148–150).

How he builds it.

  • The bus driver who said girls rejected him, though he'd turned down two himself. At 12 he'd wished his father dead before the man's heart attack. "Did you kill him, or did he have a heart attack?… You are thirty-one now, not twelve" (pp. 150–152).
  • The Memphis salesman with no hands became a top machinery-parts salesman; asked about his artificial hands: "Too damned hot" (pp. 152–153).
  • Unlocking your true personality — three steps (pp. 154–155): get in contact with your (constructive) aggressiveness; focus on your goals; accept your individuality.
  • The woman who couldn't talk set a goal of selling real estate and was a top agent in two years (pp. 156–157).

What he asks you to do. Be "the host at your feast of Thanksgiving" every day, even a bad one (pp. 158–160).

Chapter 11 — Tranquillity and Self-Respect (pp. 162–179)

What he's saying. He spells it with two l's "to get the most out of it" (p. 162). Armstrong and Aldrin landed on the Sea of Tranquillity; your trip there is free (pp. 163–165).

How he builds it — the TRANQUILLITY dozen, the ways to lose it (pp. 165–178): Tension, Resistance, Arrogant aggressiveness, Negation as a way of life, Quandary, Unbelief, Inferiority, Life of loneliness, Laissez-faire, Irritation, Trepidation, Yesterday Incorporated. Stories include:

  • The engineer with "blurred vision" whose eyes were fine; orphaned young, he had a "yesterday-tomorrow complex." "Now. Today." A year later his vision was clear (pp. 168–171).
  • The Santo Domingo beauty queen scarred in a car crash: "I thought I'd never be myself again" (pp. 175–177).
  • The woman grieving her father: "Respect the memory of your father—but live your own life today" (pp. 177–178).

How it connects. Tranquillity is active, built brick by brick, not a hammock (pp. 178–179). Goal Builder 11 uses soothing sounds.

Chapter 12 — The Active Life: No Retirement Clock (pp. 180–196)

What he's saying. The retirement clock ticks at five, fifteen or sixty-five (p. 181). Self-respect "does not mean perfection"; it means you seek, do your best, accept yourself and others, and forgive in action (p. 182).

How he builds it — the retirement clock and its alternatives (pp. 183–191): Retreat → Return (Nixon, FDR, the 1969 Mets); Eviction → Conviction; Tendency to withdraw → Courage to act; Impotence → Imaginative power (the friend whose $50 check to her hometown church bounced); Resignation → Reaffirmation ("a liquorless toast"); Evasion → Responsibility; Mañana → Today; Ennui → Emancipation (the sleeping boa constrictor); No direction → Goal orientation (Pablo Casals, born again each day); Traitor → Friend ("Befriend yourself, and never retire").

The "generationless gap." A quiet engineer and his 23-year-old son never spoke beyond hello and goodbye. There was no hate, just two shy men; Maltz told the son it was his job, as the younger, to open the line (pp. 191–195).

What he asks you to do. Accept your mistakes as "this comforting cushion" so you can come out of your shell (pp. 195–196).

Chapter 13 — Integrity as a Way of Life (pp. 197–213)

What he's saying. People know where their car keys are, but not themselves (p. 197). Integrity means wholeness; a person at war with himself must "declare a cease-fire" (p. 198). There's "grit" in integrity (p. 199).

How he builds it — the INTEGRITY acrostic (pp. 199–212): Inquiring mind; Natural good tone; Thoughtful focusing; Encountering the you in you; Goal of confidence; Reaching for reality; Industrious involvement; Tenacity in thinking; Yearning for emotional nourishment. Highlights:

  • The room experiment: study every object in a room, leave, list them, return and see what you missed. Then focus each day within, behind and before yourself (pp. 202–203).
  • Symbolic golf in your living room — no club, no ball, eye on the ball and follow through (pp. 204–205). Maltz's own career followed this: after delivering a baby with a harelip he chose plastic surgery against everyone's advice (pp. 205–206).
  • "Insincerity is the mother of exhaustion" (p. 207). The young mother who blamed herself for her daughter's cut cheek shows reality faced obsessively, but also acted on quickly (pp. 208–211).

How it connects. "Self-respect follows integrity" (p. 213). Goal Builder 13 is a list of your moments of integrity.

Chapter 14 — Survival with Self-Respect (pp. 214–229)

What he's saying. We want "a qualitative survival" (p. 214). The Wright brothers flew after Langley failed: "You, too, can fly" (pp. 215–216). Become a psycho-cybernaut, steering through inner space to the right side of the moon (pp. 216–218).

How he builds it.

  • NO SURVIVAL (pp. 218–221): Negative feelings, Oppression, Separation, Unacceptableness, Resistance to living, Vacillation, Indifference, Vulnerability, Absenteeism, Lamenting yesterday.
  • The tall midget and the "little self." A Toronto bellhop about three feet tall had a self-image "as tall as that twenty-six-foot Christmas tree"; a pretty would-be actress said "My 'little self' is killing me," played her better self in real life, and eight months later was in an off-Broadway musical (pp. 222–224).
  • SURVIVAL (pp. 224–227): Sense of forward movement, Upgrading, Relaxed attitude (forgive "not on the installment plan"), Vitality ("A near-success is a failure"), Imaginative power, Values, Aspiration for happiness, Living now.

What he asks you to do. Take the imaginary exam: would your self-respect be "invisible"? Then do the work yourself (p. 229).

Chapter 15 — Goal Builders (pp. 230–249)

What he's saying. The search is lifelong — "If you own a plant and love it, you keep watering it" (p. 249). Words help only if you use them (p. 231).

How he builds it. A fourteen-week program: one Goal Builder per week, worked six days with one day off (p. 231). Each pairs an imagined scene or written task with a word written on your mirror in lipstick or erasable pencil and a sentence said to yourself. The fourteen, in order: Self-Respect, Understanding, Sincerity, Defeating Uncertainty, Overcoming Resentment, Getting Rid of Emptiness, Opportunity, Forgiveness, Creative Mirror Watching, Tearing Down Prison Walls, Tranquillity, No Retirement Clock, Integrity, Survival (the Reference and the activity page have each).

What he asks you to do. Finish at the mirror: "As long as I live, I will strive to make myself a complete person, in my own image…" (p. 249).
