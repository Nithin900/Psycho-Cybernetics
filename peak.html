Peak

Anders Ericsson & Robert Pool · Secrets from the New Science of Expertise · 2016

What this book is

A book by the psychologist who spent more than thirty years studying the best performers in the world: chess players, violinists, athletes, doctors, memory experts. It explains how they got that good and how anyone can use the same method. Ericsson calls the method deliberate practice, a name he coined in the early 1990s (p. 10).

It is written from Ericsson's point of view. The "I" in the book is him, while Robert Pool, a science writer, co-wrote it (Authors' Note).

AuthorsAnders Ericsson, Robert Pool
Published2016 (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt; The Bodley Head in the UK)
LengthIntroduction + 9 chapters, 259 pages of text
Key ideaDeliberate practice and mental representations

The book in one sentence

Extraordinary ability is built, not born: the right kind of practice, kept up over time, reshapes the brain and body, and the most important thing it builds is better mental representations.

Ericsson's own words: "Learning isn't a way of reaching one's potential but rather a way of developing it. We can create our own potential" (p. xx). And: "The right sort of practice carried out over a sufficient period of time leads to improvement. Nothing else" (p. xxi).

"The gift"

The introduction sets up the whole argument with perfect pitch. Mozart could name any note at age seven, and only about one person in ten thousand has this ability, so it looks like a rare gift (pp. xi–xiii). But in 2014 the Japanese psychologist Ayako Sakakibara trained twenty-four children aged two to six to recognize chords, a few minutes at a time, several times a day. Every one of them developed perfect pitch (pp. xiv–xv).

So perfect pitch is not the gift. The gift is the brain's ability to develop it, and nearly everyone is born with that (p. xvi). The old view treats potential like a cup with a fixed size. Ericsson's view treats it as "an expandable vessel, shaped by the various things we do throughout our lives" (p. xx).

Three kinds of practice

The book's main tool is a ladder of practice. Most of what people call practice is on the bottom rung.

Naive practice

"I just played it." Doing something again and again and expecting to get better. Once a skill feels automatic, improvement stops, and it can slowly get worse.

pp. 11–14
Purposeful practice

Specific goals, full focus, feedback, and getting out of your comfort zone. This is how Steve Faloon went from 8 to 82 digits. It works, but on its own it can stall, as it did for Renée at about 20 digits.

pp. 14–25
Deliberate practice

Purposeful practice that "knows where it is going and how to get there." It needs a field with clear standards and a teacher who knows how experts got there. Its main product is better mental representations.

pp. 97–100

How the book is built

  1. Chapters 1–4: what deliberate practice is and why it works: purposeful practice, how the brain adapts, mental representations, and the "gold standard."
  2. Chapters 5–6: using it at work and in everyday life, including when you have no teacher.
  3. Chapter 7: the road to extraordinary: the stages people go through on the way to world-class.
  4. Chapter 8: the talent question, described by the authors as "a brief interlude" on prodigies, savants and IQ (p. xxii).
  5. Chapter 9: what's next: schools, training and a society of "Homo exercens."

Chapter by chapter

0

Introduction: The Gift

pp. xi–xxiii

Why are some people so good at what they do? Not because of a special inborn gift, but because they made more use of the gift everyone has: an adaptable brain and body (p. xviii).

  • Since the 1990s, researchers have found that even the adult brain rewires itself in response to training (p. xvi).
  • Genes do matter for a few things like height. The main gift, though, is adaptability (p. xviii).
  • Deliberate practice is "the most powerful approach to learning that has ever been discovered" (p. xxiii). Ericsson compares it to having a guide who knows the best route up a mountain.

Key stories Mozart's perfect pitch (p. xi). Sakakibara's 24 children (p. xiv). Ray Allen, the NBA's top three-point shooter, rejecting the idea that he was born with a jump shot: "Don't undermine the work I've put in every day. Not some days. Every day" (p. xviii). His high school coach says his jump shot was poor back then (p. xix).

1

The Power of Purposeful Practice

pp. 1–25

Steve Faloon, a Carnegie Mellon student, could at first hold seven or eight digits read one per second, the normal limit of short-term memory. After more than two hundred sessions he could recall 82 (pp. 1–5).

  • The training rule: get a string right, the next one is one digit longer. Get it wrong, it drops by two. That kept Steve right at the edge of his ability (p. 4).
  • Performance keeps rising across fields: the 1908 marathon record (2:55:18) would barely qualify for today's Boston Marathon; the double somersault went from "too dangerous" to an entry-level dive (p. 6).
  • The usual approach (learn until it's automatic, then stop) leads to a plateau. Doctors with twenty years in practice can be worse than recent graduates (pp. 11–13).
  • Four traits of purposeful practice: well-defined specific goals; focus; feedback; getting out of your comfort zone (pp. 15–18).
  • When you hit a wall, "the solution is not 'try harder' but rather 'try differently'" (p. 19).
  • Trying hard is not enough. Renée, a second subject, worked out her own system and stalled near 20 digits. Dario, who started from Steve's method and then built his own, passed 100 (pp. 22–25).

Key stories Ben Franklin played chess for over fifty years, sometimes from 6 p.m. until sunrise, but never got much better because he never pushed past his comfort zone (pp. 18–19). Dorothy DeLay's student, who played faster than Itzhak Perlman by raising the metronome a little at a time (p. 20).

2

Harnessing Adaptability

pp. 26–49

The brain changes with training the way muscles do. You just can't see it in the mirror.

  • London cabbies learn "the Knowledge": about 25,000 streets within six miles of Charing Cross (p. 29).
  • Eleanor Maguire found licensed cabbies had a larger posterior hippocampus. In her 2011 study the trainees who passed grew it; those who dropped out did not (pp. 30–32).
  • Middle-aged people trained their vision for three months and could read letters 60 percent smaller, without glasses (p. 36).
  • Homeostasis: the body only changes when pushed past what it can comfortably handle. Push too little and nothing changes; push too much and you burn out or get injured. The sweet spot is just outside your comfort zone (pp. 37–41).
  • Training that starts young has bigger effects ("the bent-twig effect"), skills need upkeep, and specializing can cost you elsewhere (pp. 45–47).

Key idea Traditional learning assumes you are filling a fixed potential. "With deliberate practice, however, the goal is not just to reach your potential but to build it" (p. 48).

3

Mental Representations

pp. 50–83

The center of the book. A mental representation is a structure in long-term memory that holds the patterns of a field (facts, images, rules, relationships) so you can respond quickly without overloading short-term memory (pp. 58–61).

  • Grandmasters remember real board positions almost perfectly but random ones no better than novices. They see patterns ("chunks"), roughly 50,000 of them (pp. 55–57).
  • Representations are domain-specific: "there is no such thing as developing a general skill" (p. 60).
  • "What sets expert performers apart from everyone else is the quality and quantity of their mental representations" (p. 62).
  • They help you see patterns (soccer players, quarterbacks, climbers), make sense of information (people who know baseball remember a baseball story better), find answers (a doctor linking ear pain and a small pupil to a torn artery), and plan (surgeons, climbers, writers) (pp. 63–76).
  • They help you learn: a beginner saxophonist fixed 8 in 10 of his mistakes on the second try, while a beginner cornet player fixed 3 in 10. The difference was mainly how clearly each could hear what the piece should sound like (pp. 77–78).
  • Skill and representations feed each other: "like a staircase that you climb as you build it" (p. 83).

Key stories Alekhine playing twenty-six blindfold games at once in 1924 (p. 50). Ericsson and Pool rewriting this book until mental representations became its "keystone" (pp. 73–76).

4

The Gold Standard

pp. 84–114

What separates the best from the very good? Ericsson's Berlin study of violin students answers it.

  • By age 18 the best students had averaged about 7,410 hours of solitary practice, the better ones 5,301 and the future music teachers 3,420. Nobody found the practice fun (pp. 93–94).
  • Deliberate practice adds two things to purposeful practice: a well-developed field with clear standards, and a teacher who knows how experts got there (p. 98).
  • Its seven traits are listed on pp. 99–100 (see Toolkit).
  • In fields without that structure: "get as close to deliberate practice as you can." Identify the real experts, find out what they do differently, and design training to match (p. 103).
  • Be careful naming experts. Wine judges gave the same wine scores that varied by up to four points either way (pp. 104–105).
  • The ten-thousand-hour rule is not a rule. The number isn't magic, it was an average, performing is not practice (the Beatles' Hamburg hours were closer to 1,100), and it was never a promise (pp. 109–112).

Key idea "There is no point at which performance maxes out and additional practice does not lead to further improvement" (p. 113).

5

Principles of Deliberate Practice on the Job

pp. 115–144

How do you improve people who are already trained and working?

  • Top Gun. In early 1968 Navy pilots were trading planes about one for one with North Vietnamese fighters. The Navy built a school where the best pilots played the enemy, then grilled students after every flight. From 1970 to 1973 the Navy downed 12.5 enemy planes per loss, while the Air Force stayed near 2 to 1 (pp. 115–118).
  • Art Turock's three myths: abilities are fixed by genes; doing something long enough makes you better; effort is enough (pp. 121–122).
  • "Learning while real work gets done": a presenter picks one skill to work on, the audience gives feedback. Blue Bunny rehearsed sales calls as role-plays on video (pp. 122–123).
  • The Top Gun approach elsewhere: libraries of mammograms or X-rays with known outcomes, used for practice with instant feedback (pp. 125–128).
  • Knowledge vs. skills. In a 2005 Harvard review, only 2 of 62 studies found doctors improved with experience. Lecture-style continuing education had no significant effect; interactive training helped a little (pp. 132–135).
  • Prostate surgery: cancer came back within five years in 17.9 percent of patients of surgeons with 10 prior operations, versus 10.7 percent at 250 (p. 139).

Key idea Ask "How do we improve the relevant skills?" rather than "How do we teach the relevant knowledge?" (p. 137).

6

Principles of Deliberate Practice in Everyday Life

pp. 145–179

For anyone who wants to improve at anything: golf, karate, singing, writing, code.

  • Find a good teacher, someone accomplished who also knows how to teach. Look for evidence of students' progress, not fun lessons (pp. 147–150).
  • Stay engaged. Group classes make it easy to just go through the motions. "It is better to train at 100 percent effort for less time than at 70 percent effort for a longer period" (pp. 150–154).
  • No teacher? Use the three Fs: "Focus. Feedback. Fix it" (p. 159). Ben Franklin taught himself to write by rewriting essays from The Spectator from his notes and comparing them with the originals (pp. 155–157).
  • Plateaus: push yourself a little harder than usual to see what breaks first, then design practice aimed at that one weakness (pp. 161–165).
  • Motivation: willpower is not a general trait. Instead, weaken the reasons to quit and strengthen the reasons to keep going (pp. 166–178).

Key stories Dan McLaughlin, a 30-year-old photographer who had never played a round of golf, got his handicap down to 3–4 (pp. 145, 178). Per Holmlöv started karate at 69 (p. 147). Natalie Coughlin stopped daydreaming during laps and focused on every stroke (pp. 152–153). Josh Foer broke his card-memory plateau and won the 2006 USA Memory Championship (p. 161).

7

The Road to Extraordinary

pp. 180–206

László and Klara Polgár set out to prove any child could become a genius, and chose chess because it "is very objective and easy to measure" (p. 181). All three daughters became top players. Judit became a grandmaster at fifteen (pp. 180–182).

Benjamin Bloom's study of 120 experts shows the same stages everywhere (pp. 184–194):

  1. Playful start. The field is a toy; parents and older siblings spark interest.
  2. Becoming serious. Lessons, a daily routine; motivation moves from parents to the child; by 11–12 they call themselves "pianists" or "swimmers."
  3. Commitment. In the mid-teens: the best teachers, more hours, large costs.
  4. Pathbreaking. A few go beyond what anyone has done. They get there in small steps that look like leaps from outside (pp. 202–206).
  • Starting late: some physical traits need childhood training, but adults can still learn a great deal. Paul Brady taught himself perfect pitch at 32 in two months of daily half-hour sessions (pp. 194–201).
8

But What About Natural Talent?

pp. 207–242

Ericsson's test for any "natural" ability: what exactly is the ability, and what training made it possible? (p. 211)

  • Paganini's breaking-string act was planned: he wrote pieces to finish on one string and practised them (pp. 208–211).
  • Mozart's early works are in his father's handwriting. His first certainly original serious compositions came at 15–16, after a decade of training (pp. 211–214).
  • Donald Thomas, the "untrained" high jumper, had used the Fosbury Flop in high school and trained his jump by dunking. Despite years of coaching afterward, he never beat the 2.35 m he cleared in 2007 (pp. 215–219).
  • Savants like Donny, the calendar calculator, built their skill through obsessive practice. A graduate student matched calendar-calculating twins after 16 sessions (pp. 220–222).
  • IQ and chess: IQ helps beginners. But among 23 elite young players, those with lower IQs were slightly better, because they practised more (pp. 227–233).
  • No one can predict who will reach the top. Tom Brady went 199th in the draft (p. 236).
  • The dark side: believing in talent becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy, as with Canadian hockey players born early in the year (pp. 238–242).
9

Where Do We Go from Here?

pp. 243–259

A glimpse of the future at the University of British Columbia. Two instructors who had never taught a class used methods based on deliberate practice for one week of a physics course. Their students scored 74 percent on the test; the section taught by an experienced professor scored 41 percent (pp. 243–247).

  • Start a lesson with what students should be able to do, not what they should know. The knowledge "comes along for the ride" (p. 251).
  • Teach step by step, just outside the comfort zone, with lots of repetition and feedback (p. 253).
  • Every student should develop expertise in at least one area, to learn what expertise takes (p. 255).
  • Ericsson proposes a new name for us: Homo exercens, "practicing man" (p. 258).

Toolkit

Every method and checklist the book gives, in a form you can use without the book open.

Purposeful practice: four checks

pp. 15–18
  1. A well-defined, specific goal for this session, not "get better."
  2. Full focus. No mind-wandering.
  3. Feedback on every attempt: what went right, what went wrong, and why.
  4. Just outside your comfort zone. Something you can't quite do yet.

Get outside your comfort zone but do it in a focused way, with clear goals, a plan for reaching those goals, and a way to monitor your progress. (p. 22)

Deliberate practice: seven traits

pp. 99–100
  1. It builds skills others have already figured out how to do, using proven training methods, under a teacher or coach.
  2. It takes place outside your comfort zone and demands near-maximum effort. It's usually not enjoyable.
  3. It has well-defined, specific goals, broken into a plan of small changes.
  4. It is deliberate: full attention and conscious control.
  5. It involves feedback and changing what you do in response. Over time you learn to give yourself feedback.
  6. It both builds and depends on effective mental representations.
  7. It builds on existing skills step by step, so the fundamentals must be right from the start.

When strict deliberate practice isn't possible

pp. 103–108
  1. Identify the expert performers, using objective measures where you can. Be wary of reputation, seniority and charm.
  2. No objective measure? Ask people who see many professionals at work (like a nurse on several surgical teams), or find who professionals call when they're stuck.
  3. Figure out what they do differently, or what their training does differently.
  4. Design practice that lets you do it too. Keep what works; drop what doesn't.
  5. Whenever possible, work with a good coach.

Stay at the edge: the +1 / −2 rule

p. 4
  1. Start at a level you can manage.
  2. Succeed: make the next attempt one step harder.
  3. Fail: make it two steps easier.

This is how Steve Faloon was "constantly challenged, but not too much."

No teacher: the three Fs

p. 159

Focus. Feedback. Fix it.

  1. Break the skill into parts you can repeat and analyze.
  2. Find your weaknesses.
  3. Figure out ways to fix them, then repeat.

Creative versions from the book: ask strangers the same question again and again to hear the answers repeatedly (ESL student); keep strangers talking at rush hour and time yourself (circus ringmaster); try material at open-mic nights (comedians) (pp. 158–159).

Franklin's method: learn from a model

pp. 155–156
  1. Pick work by someone excellent.
  2. Write short hints about each part, just enough to remind you what it says.
  3. Wait a few days, then reproduce it from your hints.
  4. Compare with the original and correct your version.
  5. Variations: rewrite it in a different form and back (he turned prose into verse) to build range; shuffle your hints and rebuild the order to learn structure.

The same works for chess (replay grandmaster games), art (copy the masters) and music (Mozart studied great composers' work) (p. 160).

Choose a good teacher

pp. 147–150
  • Accomplished in the field, though not necessarily world-class. They can only take you as far as they or their students have gone.
  • Experienced at teaching. Many great performers are poor teachers.
  • Talk to their students, especially ones who started at your level and age.
  • Ignore reviews about fun; look for evidence of progress.
  • Ask how they guide solo practice: what to work on, what to watch for, how to recognize good performance.
  • When you stop improving, look for a new teacher.

Stay engaged

pp. 150–154, 170–171
  • Shorter sessions with clearer goals. 100 percent effort beats 70 percent for longer.
  • About an hour per session. Take a break if you want more.
  • End the session when you can no longer focus.
  • Sleep enough. The Berlin violinists napped.
  • Even in repetitive work (laps, reps, scales), attend to every detail of form.

Break a plateau

pp. 161–165
  1. Challenge yourself in a new way: change the exercise, load or routine.
  2. Still stuck? Push yourself a little (not a lot) harder than usual: faster, a tougher opponent, a busier day.
  3. Watch what breaks first. That part is holding you back.
  4. Design practice aimed only at that weakness (typists drill "ol" words).
  5. Other moves from Faloon's study: slow down to see whether the problem is speed; try far beyond your limit to show it's possible (pp. 20–21).

Keep going: motivation

pp. 166–178

Weaken reasons to quit

  • A fixed practice time, cleared of everything else.
  • Remove distractions: phone off and in another room.
  • Sleep, health, sessions of about an hour.

Strengthen reasons to keep going

  • Let the skill become part of who you are.
  • Believe you can improve. After a setback, agree to get back to where you were before you decide to quit (p. 173).
  • Join or build a group with the same goals (Franklin's Junto).
  • Break the journey into small, measurable levels, with small rewards. Dan McLaughlin's putting game logged 102 putts per game (p. 177).

Top Gun debrief

p. 117

After each attempt, answer:

  1. What did you notice?
  2. What actions did you take?
  3. Why did you choose to do that?
  4. What were your mistakes?
  5. What could you have done differently?

Then try again the next day. Over time you start asking yourself these questions.

Judge any training program

p. 124
  1. Does it push people outside their comfort zone?
  2. Does it give immediate feedback and say how to improve?
  3. Did its designers identify the best performers and what sets them apart?
  4. Does it develop the specific skills those experts have?

Practise while working

pp. 122–123
  1. Before a presentation, the speaker picks one skill to work on (stories, speaking without slides).
  2. The audience takes notes on how it went and practises giving feedback.
  3. For a big sales call: role-play it, get feedback, record it, rehearse again the next day.
  4. Make it routine, not a one-off.

Test a "talent" story

pp. 211, 217
  1. What exactly is the ability?
  2. What training could have produced it?

Ericsson says thirty years of asking these two questions never turned up a case they couldn't explain.

Design a lesson the UBC way

pp. 250–253
  1. Decide what learners should be able to do. Make objectives skills, not knowledge.
  2. Study how experts do it, especially their mental representations.
  3. Teach it step by step, each step just outside the comfort zone.
  4. Give lots of repetition and feedback: try, fail, get feedback, try again.
