Study notes · Maxwell Maltz (1960)

Change the image,
change the life.

Maltz was a plastic surgeon who noticed that fixing a face didn't always fix how people felt. His conclusion: behaviour follows the self-image, and the brain is a goal-seeking machine that steers toward whatever target that image sets.

From theory to practice

Tap a principle to open it. Left: why it works. Right: what to do.

1

The self-image is the key

Theory

Some surgery patients transformed; others still felt ugly with a fixed face. The outer change only worked when the inner picture changed too.

Everyone carries a picture of "what kind of person I am," built from past wins, failures and how others reacted. We treat it as fact and act consistently with it. Behaviour follows the self-image — fighting it with effort alone is fighting your own steering wheel.

Practice

  • Write "I'm the kind of person who…" for work, money, relationships, health.
  • Trace each limiting line to where it came from — often one incident or comment.
  • Write a new, realistic present-tense self-description.
  • Aim for an accurate image, not an inflated fantasy.
2

The success mechanism

Theory

From cybernetics: a guided missile has a target, moves, gets feedback, corrects, repeats. It succeeds by making and correcting errors.

Your brain works the same way, mostly below awareness. The mechanism is neutral — it steers just as efficiently toward failure if that's the target your self-image sets.

Practice

  • Give it a clear, picturable target.
  • Focus on the what; let the how unfold.
  • After a mistake ask "what adjustment does this tell me?" — then drop it.
  • Think a problem through before sleep, then stop and let the subconscious work.
3

Imagination is the steering wheel

Theory

We react to what we believe or imagine to be true — imagined danger produces real fear. Worry is just negative imagination, rehearsing failure. The same process can rehearse success.

Practice — Theater of the Mind

  • 30 quiet minutes a day.
  • Picture yourself acting and feeling the way you want in one specific situation.
  • Add sensory detail: room, sounds, faces.
  • Focus on the feeling (calm, capable), not just the outcome.
  • Repeat daily for at least three weeks.
4

Dehypnotize yourself

Theory

A hypnotized person told "you can't lift this pencil" truly can't. Many people are "hypnotized" by beliefs accepted in childhood. Maltz's example: Alfred Adler was told he was bad at math, believed it, did poorly — until he solved a problem no one else could, and the belief cracked.

Practice

  • List your "I can't" statements.
  • Find exceptions — one is enough to show it isn't fixed.
  • Run a small, low-risk experiment against the belief.
5

Rational thinking, not willpower

Theory

When willpower and imagination fight, imagination wins. Conscious thinking's job is to choose goals, check beliefs and feed accurate data to the automatic mechanism — then step back. Strong desire for the new result gives the goal energy.

Practice

  • Run the four rational questions on any negative belief.
  • Use past failures as feedback only, not identity.
  • Pair reasons with genuine feeling about the change.
6

Relax and let it work

Theory

Over-trying jams the mechanism — like a trembling hand missing the needle's eye. Good performance comes when you're relaxed and absorbed, not tense and self-watching.

Practice

  • Do your thinking before the action; trust yourself during it.
  • Physically relax: lie down, let the body go heavy.
  • Keep worry time separate from action time.
7

Happiness is a habit

Theory

Happiness is mostly a mental habit, not a result of circumstances. "I'll be happy when…" postpones it forever. A happy mind also thinks and performs better, so happiness is a condition for success, not just a reward.

Practice

  • Ask: does this deserve my peace of mind?
  • Notice small pleasures daily.
  • Drop "I'll be happy when…"
  • Act cheerful — feelings follow actions.
8

The success-type personality

Theory

Traits of people whose mechanism works well, summed up as SUCCESS. Confidence is built on remembering successes, not failures.

Practice

  • Rate yourself 1–10 on each trait; pick one or two.
  • Keep a "success file" of wins; read it before challenges.
  • Do one slightly uncomfortable thing daily.
  • Separate who you are from what you did.
9

The failure mechanism

Theory

Warning signs summed up as FAILURE. They're dashboard lights, not identity — signals that something needs correcting.

Practice

  • Name it: "this is resentment."
  • Trace it: which goal is blocked, which belief is involved?
  • Redirect: channel frustration toward the real goal; decide to ease uncertainty; set a goal to fill emptiness.
10

The emotional facelift

Theory

Physical wounds leave thick, insensitive scar tissue; emotional hurt does the same. Defences built against pain also block closeness and creativity. The cure is real forgiveness — cancelling the debt because carrying it harms you.

Practice

  • List grudges and regrets you still carry.
  • Cancel the debt — no need to excuse or reconcile.
  • Forgive yourself specifically: name it, take the lesson, release it.
  • Build resilience, not walls.
11

Release your real personality

Theory

Inhibition is excessive negative feedback. Too much self-monitoring freezes the system — the stutterer who gets worse the harder they watch themselves.

Practice

  • Think before acting, not while acting.
  • Speak a bit louder and a bit sooner; greet people first.
  • Accept occasional awkwardness — nobody watches as closely as you think.
  • Shift attention outward, to the person or task.
12

Do-it-yourself tranquilizers

Theory

We react automatically, like rushing to a ringing phone. You can learn to delay the response, and build an inner place of calm.

Practice

  • Build a mental "quiet room"; visit it a few minutes daily and before stress.
  • Pause a few seconds before reacting.
  • Clear yourself between activities so stress doesn't spill over.
13

Crisis into opportunity

Theory

People who perform under pressure usually practiced without pressure first. The realistic worst case is usually smaller than the fear.

Practice

  • Rehearse when nothing is at stake.
  • Name the worst case and how you'd handle it.
  • Read the adrenaline as excitement.
  • Keep the goal, not the danger, in focus.
14

The winning feeling

Theory

When the mechanism is on track there's a recognizable feeling of calm confidence. You can call it up deliberately before acting.

Practice

  • Recall a past success in detail, especially how it felt.
  • Carry that feeling into the new challenge.
  • Act "as if" — posture, voice, preparation.
15

The 21-day principle

Theory

Patients took about 21 days to get used to a new face; amputees about the same to lose a phantom limb. Maltz meant this as a minimum for a new image to set in — not "habits take 21 days."

Practice

  • Commit to one change for at least 21 days.
  • Expect the old image to push back early.
  • Track each day's practice.
  • Plan for longer — see the evidence check.

A daily routine

Most of the principles, applied together. Run it for 21 days, then keep going.

Morning · 5 min
Read your new self-description. Pick one goal for the day.
During the day
Watch for FAILURE signals — name, trace, redirect. Pause before reacting. Do one small brave thing.
Before challenges · 2 min
Visit your quiet room, then recall the winning feeling from a past success.
Evening · 30 min
Theater of the Mind. Add today's wins to your success file. Forgive one thing — including your own mistakes.
Before sleep
Think a problem through clearly, then hand it to your subconscious.

The four rational questions

From Chapter 5, "How to Utilize the Power of Rational Thinking" — rewritten in plain first-person language. Use them in order whenever an "I can't" thought shows up.

Why do I actually believe this?Is there a rational basis for it, or did I just absorb it?
Could I be wrong about myself here?Look for exceptions and contrary evidence.
Would I judge a friend this harshly?Apply the standard you'd use for someone else in the same situation.
If it's not true, why am I still living like it is?Turn the conclusion into a change in behaviour.

Evidence check

Read Maltz as a motivational text, not a science textbook. Keep the tools; skip the absolute claims.

Holds up reasonably well

  • Self-image shapes behaviour
  • Mental rehearsal helps — especially combined with real practice
  • How you explain setbacks matters (echoed in Seligman's optimism research)
  • Self-compassion beats harsh self-criticism

Outdated or overstated

  • "21 days to form a habit" — research suggests about 66 days on average, with wide variation
  • Left-brain / right-brain claims in some companion material — not supported by modern neuroscience
  • "Strong visualization prevents failure" — Oettingen's research shows pure positive fantasy can reduce effort; pair it with mental contrasting
