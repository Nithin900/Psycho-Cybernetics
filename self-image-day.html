Self-Image Day

Maltz proposes a day when “you pull your name off the Loser’s List and you tack it up on the Winner’s List.” This page turns it into a daily practice of seven exercises, all taken from the book, morning to night.

Morning mirror

“…when you get up in the morning and look at yourself in the mirror. You ask yourself who you want to be that day? You must answer” (p. 209). Then set the day’s goal: “You wake up in the morning and you set a goal for that day” (p. 14).

2

Off the Loser’s List

On Self-Image Day you are a friend to yourself, and the only present is a roundtable discussion with your self-image (p. 17).

      3

      The double feature

      “Close your eyes, relax, and get ready for the double feature in the theater of your mind.” Two close-ups, and the strange thing is that both of them are you (pp. 31–32). Close your eyes, see both, then choose which picture runs today.

      4

      Your self image, spelled out

      The 13 “dual forces in you” (pp. 23–37): to be happy, you must bolster the positive forces and fight off the desire for defeat and negation. For each pair, tap the side you lived today. Be honest; this is for you.

        5

        Four points against a resentment

        What Maltz told the woman who resented her mother: Four points… none of them easy — unless you have the desire to make them easy (p. 29). Pick one grievance you carried today and work through each point.

        6

        Two quiet minutes

        Pick one. Your own Kentucky Derby: Every day we allot two minutes to ourselves… We see ourselves running for our self-respect (p. 242). Or the geyser: see a geyser letting off steam, a symbol for you to let go your tensions of the day (pp. 69, 206).

        Evening: recall and verdict

        “When you hit the pillow at night, you recall how during the day you gave your self-image a boost. Recall this — placidly, pleasantly” (p. 14). Then be a wise and compassionate judge, and pass a gentle judgment upon yourself (p. 61).

        NOT GUILTY

        “Not guilty of being a criminal. Not guilty of being inferior. Not guilty of being an unworthy human being who deserves punishment” (p. 61).
