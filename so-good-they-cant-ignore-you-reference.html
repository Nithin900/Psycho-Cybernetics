Laws, theories and traps

Each one in the book’s own words.

Name The wording Pages
The passion hypothesis “The key to occupational happiness is to first figure out what you’re passionate about and then find a job that matches this passion.” 16–17
The career capital theory of great work The traits that define great work are rare and valuable. · Supply and demand says that if you want these traits you need rare and valuable skills to offer in return. · Think of these rare and valuable skills you can offer as your career capital. · The craftsman mindset, with its relentless focus on becoming “so good they can’t ignore you,” is a strategy well suited for acquiring career capital. 53
The 10,000-hour rule “Excellence at performing a complex task requires a critical minimum level of practice… ten thousand hours” (Gladwell’s summary) 79
The first control trap “Control that’s acquired without career capital is not sustainable.” 109
The second control trap “The point at which you have acquired enough career capital to get meaningful control over your working life is exactly the point when you’ve become valuable enough to your current employer that they will try to prevent you from making the change.” 118
The law of financial viability “When deciding whether to follow an appealing pursuit that will introduce more control into your work life, seek evidence of whether people are willing to pay for it. If you find this evidence, continue. If not, move on.” 125
The law of remarkability “For a mission-driven project to succeed, it should be remarkable in two different ways. First, it must compel people who encounter it to remark about it to others. Second, it must be launched in a venue that supports such remarking.” 168
Think small, act big “Advancing to the cutting edge in a field is an act of ‘small’ thinking… Once you get to the cutting edge, however, and discover a mission in the adjacent possible, you must go after it with zeal: a ‘big’ action.” 148

Numbered lists

List The items Pages
Three conclusions from the science of passion Career passions are rare · passion takes time · passion is a side effect of mastery 26–29
Job, career, calling (Wrzesniewski) A job pays the bills · a career is a path toward increasingly better work · a calling is an important part of your life and a vital part of your identity 27
Three needs (Self-Determination Theory) Autonomy: control over your day, and your actions matter · Competence: you are good at what you do · Relatedness: connection to other people 29
Two reasons against the passion mindset It makes you hyperaware of what you don’t like · its deep questions (“Who am I?”) are impossible to confirm 46
Traits that define great work Creativity · Impact · Control (Mission is added in Rule #4) 50, 136
Three disqualifiers for the craftsman mindset Few chances to build rare and valuable skills · the job is useless or bad for the world · you must work with people you really dislike 59
Two parts of deliberate practice Stretch past what’s comfortable · honest, immediate feedback 76, 92
Five habits of a craftsman Decide what capital market you’re in · identify your capital type · define “good” · stretch and destroy · be patient 87–94
Two career capital markets Winner-take-all: one type of capital, many competitors · Auction: many types of capital, each person’s mix can be unique 88
Three tactics for mission Build career capital · use little bets · use a marketing mindset (the law of remarkability) 137
Two types of structure for hard focus Time structure (“for the next hour this is my whole world”) · information structure (proof map → self-quizzes → proof summaries) 181
Three deliberate-practice routines Research bible · hour tally · theory notebook 183–185
A little bet (Newport’s version) Small enough to finish in under a month · forces you to create new value · produces a concrete result for concrete feedback 194
The mission pyramid Top: tentative research mission · Middle: exploratory projects (little bets) · Bottom: background research 191–195

Key terms

Term What Newport means Pages
Passion hypothesis Find your passion first, then a job to match it; he argues it is wrong and dangerous 16–17, 214
Craftsman mindset An output-centric approach: focus on the value you offer the world 44, 214
Passion mindset Focus on the value your job offers you; leads to “chronic dissatisfaction” 46, 214
Career capital Rare and valuable skills; “the key currency for creating work you love” 53, 214
“The tape doesn’t lie” Studio musicians’ adage: on playback, “your ability has no hiding place” 44
Argument from pre-existing passion The objection that only the already-passionate can adopt the craftsman mindset 47–48
Cloud of external distractions Jordan Tice’s name for worrying about how his career compares with his friends’ 48
Courage culture Authors and commentators who say only courage stands between you and a dream job 56, 119, 216
Deliberate practice Ericsson: “activity designed, typically by a teacher, for the sole purpose of effectively improving specific aspects of an individual’s performance” 81, 215
Acceptable level Where performance plateaus if you just show up and work hard 82, 180
Winner-take-all market One type of career capital and many competitors (TV writing, advice blogging) 88, 216
Auction market Many types of career capital; each person builds a unique mix (cleantech) 88, 216
Open gates Chances to build capital that are already open to you 90
Stretch The strain of deliberate practice, “as if my neurons are physically re-forming” 92
Diligence Martin’s sense: the willingness to ignore other pursuits that distract you 94
Control Having a say in what you do and how you do it 103, 216
Dream-job elixir Newport’s name for control 103
ROWE Results-Only Work Environment: “No results, no job” 104
Lifestyle design Timothy Ferriss’s term for designing your own path through life 110
Lone nut / first follower Sivers: “the first follower transforms the lone nut into a leader” 122
Money as a neutral indicator of value Sivers: “By aiming to make money, you’re aiming to be valuable” 124
Mission A unifying focus for your career; more general than a job; answers “What should I do with my life?” 136, 217
Multiples The same discovery made by several people at nearly the same time 141
Adjacent possible Johnson (from Kauffman): the space just beyond the cutting edge, where new combinations of existing ideas lie 141–142, 217
Little bets Sims: small experiments that learn from “little failures” and “small but significant wins” 157, 217
Purple cow / brown cow Godin: the remarkable versus the boring 165, 167
Research bible A computer document where Newport summarizes a relevant paper every week 183
Hour tally A sheet behind his desk counting the hours of deliberate practice each month 184
Theory notebook A $45 archival lab notebook for formally recording brainstorm results by hand 185
Productivity-centric vs craft-centric Getting things done vs getting better at what you do 185
