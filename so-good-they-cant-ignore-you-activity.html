Craftsman’s Day

A daily page built from the book’s own practices: the craftsman’s question, a clear “good,” an hour of strain, honest feedback and the hour tally. As Newport puts it, You stretch yourself, day after day, month after month, before finally looking up and realizing, ‘Hey, I’ve become pretty good, and people are starting to notice’ (p. 94).

Start with the craftsman’s question

Two ways to think about work: the craftsman mindset focuses on what you can offer the world; the passion mindset focuses instead on what the world can offer you (p. 47). Which question are you working from today?

2

Define “good”

Pick the skill: in a winner-take-all market there is only one; in an auction market, look for open gates (pp. 88–90). Then set the goal, because deliberate practice requires good goals (p. 91).

3

One hour of strain

Newport’s time structure: I am going to work on this for one hour… for the next hour this is my whole world (p. 181). If you’re not uncomfortable, then you’re probably stuck at an ‘acceptable level’ (p. 92). Capture it in an information structure: a map, then quizzes, then a summary in your own words (p. 181).

Stretch and destroy: get feedback

The other half of deliberate practice is embracing honest feedback—even if it destroys what you thought was good (p. 92). Your opinion isn’t what counts (Colvin, p. 92).

Newport keeps a sheet behind his desk with a row for each month and a tally of hours spent in deliberate practice: By having these hour counts stare me in the face every day I’m motivated to find new ways to fit more deliberate practice into my schedule (p. 184).

    Before a bigger move

    Not daily, but keep these to hand when you’re weighing a change.

    Should I stay and get good here? Leave if the job has any of the three disqualifiers (p. 59):

    1. Few chances to build rare and valuable skills.
    2. It focuses on something you think is useless or bad for the world.
    3. It forces you to work with people you really dislike.

    Is it time to ask for more control? The law of financial viability: seek evidence of whether people are willing to pay for it. If you find this evidence, continue. If not, move on (p. 125). Paying can mean customers, a loan, an investment or an employer saying yes (p. 125).

    Is this a good little bet? Newport’s three tests (p. 194):

    • Small enough to finish in under a month.
    • It forces you to create new value.
    • It produces a concrete result you can get feedback on.

    Is the project remarkable? It must make people remark about it, and launch in a venue that supports remarking (p. 168).
