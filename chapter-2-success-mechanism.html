Psycho-Cybernetics — Chapter 2 Report: Discovering the Success Mechanism Within You

2026-09-29 · Nithin

Overview

Chapter 2 argues that every person carries a built-in, goal-seeking "success mechanism": the brain and nervous system acting as an automatic guidance system that steers toward whatever target it is given. Maltz borrows the new science of cybernetics to explain how this mechanism works, how it corrects errors, how it finds answers, and why it succeeds or fails depending on the goals and self-picture we feed it.

Scope of this report. This report covers Chapter 2 only. It includes the links Maltz himself draws in this chapter back to the self-image from the previous chapter, and every practical suggestion he gives in this chapter. No material is brought in from other chapters.

Edition note. Section headings and some examples differ between the 1960 original and later updated editions. This report follows the chapter's core argument, which is the same across editions, and is written in paraphrase rather than quotation.

1. The success instinct

Maltz opens with animals, which achieve their goals with remarkable precision and no formal training. A squirrel gathers and stores nuts for a winter it has never experienced. Birds build nests without instruction and migrate thousands of miles to places they have never seen. We call this "instinct," but Maltz frames it as an internal guidance system that steers the creature toward what it needs to survive.

Humans have a success instinct too, but a far more flexible one. An animal's goals are fixed in advance: survival and reproduction, pursued through pre-set patterns. A human being has something the animal lacks, creative imagination, which lets a person choose goals. So the human mechanism is not limited to survival; it can be aimed at emotional, social, creative and professional goals as well.

Core claim of the chapter: you do not have to build this mechanism. It is already inside you, and your job is to understand it, give it a proper goal and let it work.

2. "You are not a machine, but you have one"

Maltz draws on cybernetics, the science of control and communication developed by Norbert Wiener and others, which studies self-guiding machines called servo-mechanisms. Engineers built target-seeking devices by copying principles found in living nervous systems. Maltz reverses the lens: studying these machines helps us understand how the human brain and nervous system operate.

His central distinction is careful:

  • The brain and nervous system form a machine — an automatic, goal-striving servo-mechanism that operates largely below conscious awareness.

  • The person is not that machine. The "you" is the operator who sets the goals, directs the mechanism and uses it. Maltz stresses this so the idea is not read as reducing people to machines.

The mechanism is impersonal: like any machine, it does not judge the goals it is given. It will work toward success or toward failure with the same efficiency, depending on the target.

3. Two types of servo-mechanisms

Maltz divides goal-seeking mechanisms into two types, and shows that the human brain performs both.

Type 1Type 2
TargetKnown in advanceNot yet known; must be found
TaskReach the targetDiscover the answer or target
Machine exampleSelf-guided torpedo or interceptor missileElectronic "brain" (computer) searching stored data
Human exampleReaching for a pen on a desk; walking to a doorRecalling a forgotten name; solving a problem

Reaching for the pen. When you pick up a pen, you do not consciously calculate which muscles to fire or by how much. You hold the goal ("pick up the pen") and the mechanism handles the path, making tiny corrections along the way. Maltz notes that an infant reaching for a rattle shows the same process before it is learned: wild overshoots, gradually refined into smooth, accurate movement.

Recalling a name. When a name slips your mind, the brain scans its stored memory until it matches the target, like a computer searching its records. Often the name surfaces later, when you have stopped trying consciously — evidence the search runs automatically.

4. How it works: negative feedback and trial and error

The mechanism reaches its goal not by being right the first time, but by moving forward, making errors, and correcting them. Maltz uses the self-guided torpedo: it heads toward the target, drifts off course, receives a signal that it is off, and corrects. It zigzags its way to the hit. This error signal is called negative feedback — information that says "off course," not "stop."

The cycle, as the chapter describes it:

  1. A goal is set.

  2. The mechanism acts toward it.

  3. Feedback reports errors (too far left, too short, wrong word).

  4. The course is corrected, and action continues.

  5. When the goal is hit, the successful response is remembered and the errors are forgotten.

  6. Next time, the mechanism replays the remembered success.

Learning a skill. Maltz applies this to all skill learning: a child learning to walk, a player learning to shoot a basketball, anyone learning a new movement. Early attempts miss; each miss supplies correction data; eventually a successful attempt occurs and is stored. Skill is the result of "remembering the hit" and dropping the misses.

Key implication: errors are not failure; they are a necessary part of the steering process. Movement plus correction is how the mechanism is designed to function. A mechanism that never moves for fear of error never reaches anything.

5. Memory, creativity and "hunches"

A vast stored memory. Maltz describes the brain as storing an enormous record of past experiences, which the mechanism draws on. He cites neurosurgical findings (associated with Dr. Wilder Penfield) that stimulating parts of the brain could bring back past experiences in vivid detail, as support for the idea that experience is recorded and retrievable.

Better than any computer. The human mechanism is presented as far superior to the "electronic brains" of the day: more complex, more flexible, and able to work on problems that are only partly defined.

The creative mechanism. The same Type 2 search process underlies creative work and problem solving. Maltz points out that inventors, scientists, writers and business people commonly report that solutions come as sudden hunches or flashes of insight, often after they have stopped consciously working on the problem — while resting, sleeping on it, or doing something else. His explanation: the conscious mind defines the problem and gathers the facts; the automatic mechanism then does the searching and delivers the answer.

A wider source of ideas. Maltz also speculates, more tentatively, that the mechanism may draw on sources of knowledge beyond an individual's personal memory, referring to research on extrasensory perception of his era. He presents this as an open possibility rather than settled fact.

6. The link to the self-image: success or failure mechanism

Because the mechanism is impersonal, it serves whatever goal it is handed. Maltz's point in this chapter is that the goals it pursues are shaped by the picture a person holds of themselves and their situation. Feed it a target of success and it steers toward success; feed it a picture of inadequacy, worry or defeat, and it will work just as efficiently to bring that about.

This is why Maltz calls it a success mechanism or a failure mechanism: the machinery is the same; only the target differs. Chronic worry, in this framing, is effectively holding a negative outcome up as a goal and letting the mechanism rehearse it.

How Chapter 2 connects back to the self-image (Chapter 1). Maltz presents this chapter as the explanation of why the self-image has the power he described earlier. The self-image sets the goals and the limits within which the automatic mechanism operates. The mechanism carries out whatever fits the picture you hold of yourself.

The chain he draws is:

  1. Your self-image determines what you believe is possible for you.

  2. Those beliefs become the targets handed to the mechanism.

  3. The mechanism then works automatically to reach those targets — successful ones or failing ones.

  4. So your behaviour, feelings and results stay consistent with your self-image.

For Maltz, cybernetics gives the self-image idea a scientific footing. It explains why people act in line with their self-image even against their conscious wishes, and why changing the self-image changes results. It also means you are not stuck with old targets: as the operator, you can consciously choose the goals and pictures you give the mechanism.

7. How to let the success mechanism work for you

The chapter closes with practical principles that follow from how the mechanism operates:

  1. You must have a goal or target to shoot at. The mechanism is goal-striving. Without a clearly defined target it has nothing to steer toward. Maltz advises picturing the goal as already existing — either in actual or potential form — so the mechanism can work toward it.

  2. Focus on the end result; the mechanism supplies the means. The mechanism is teleological: it works from the goal, not from a plan of steps. Your job is the "what"; its job is the "how." Do not be put off because you cannot yet see how you will get there.

  3. Do not be afraid of making mistakes or of temporary failure. Every servo-mechanism reaches its goal by going forward, erring and correcting. Mistakes are the feedback it needs.

  4. Once corrected, forget the error. Use a mistake to correct course, then let it go. Dwelling on past errors after they have been corrected keeps feeding the wrong target. Remember the successful responses instead.

  5. Skill learning is trial and error — remember the hit. Keep practising, note the successful attempt, and let the mechanism reproduce it.

  6. Act; don't wait for certainty. Correction only works on a moving mechanism. You must take action before you can be steered.

  7. For problems and ideas, define, gather facts, then let go. Consciously state the problem and collect the information, then stop straining. Answers and hunches tend to arrive when the mind is relaxed or busy with something else.

  8. Trust your creative mechanism and do not jam it. Too much conscious effort, over-control and anxious worry interfere with its automatic working. Do your part, then let it work rather than forcing it.

Five takeaways

  • Success-seeking is built in; you operate the mechanism rather than build it.

  • The mechanism is neutral and pursues whatever goal it is given.

  • Mistakes are steering data, not verdicts.

  • Set the goal consciously; let the "how" be worked out automatically.

  • Strain and worry jam the mechanism; relaxed trust frees it.

Maltz's action checklist from this chapter

  • Set one clear goal and picture it as already achieved or achievable

  • Leave the "how" to the mechanism; don't wait for a full plan

  • Take action, expecting errors along the way

  • Correct each error, then let it go

  • Remember and repeat your successful responses

  • For a problem: define it, gather facts, then relax and let the answer come

  • Stop over-trying and worrying; trust the mechanism

  • Check that the goals you give it match a self-image of success, not failure

Critical notes: the cybernetic model is a metaphor from 1960-era engineering, not a modern neuroscience account. The extrasensory speculation has not held up scientifically. The practical ideas — goal-setting, learning through feedback, avoiding over-control — remain broadly consistent with later research on skill learning and performance.
