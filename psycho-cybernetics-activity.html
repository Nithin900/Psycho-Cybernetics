21-Day Self-Image Practice

Maltz asks you to "reserve judgment for 21 days" while you practise (p. xiii). This page strings his exercises into one day, morning to night. Come back each day; the tracker at the bottom counts toward 21.

1

Tie your shoes a new way

Put on the other shoe first and tie your laces differently, and let that remind you to change one way of thinking today: I am beginning the day in a new and better way (p. 109).

New day, new way

Then for today: as cheerful as possible, a little friendlier, a little less critical, acting as if success were inevitable, smiling at least three times (pp. 109–110).

2

Theater of your mind

Sit alone, relax, close your eyes and watch yourself on a large screen acting, feeling, 'being,' as you want to be. Notice small details: sounds, smells, textures (pp. 45–46). Maltz asks for 30 minutes; start shorter if you need to.

Dehypnotize one belief

Find one "I can't" and ask Why? Then put it through Maltz's four questions. Don't just pass these questions by casually. Wrestle with them (p. 72).

4

SUCCESS or FAILURE today

Maltz spells the success-type personality S-U-C-C-E-S-S (p. 113) and the failure signals F-A-I-L-U-R-E (p. 130). For each line, tap the side you leaned toward today. A failure light is just a dashboard signal: glance at it and steer back (p. 147).

    5

    Let the telephone ring

    Think of one "bell" today: a remark, a delay, someone's mood. The telephone is ringing, but I do not have to answer it. I can just let it ring (p. 190). What did you do?

    6

    Your quiet room

    Build a room in your mind: restful colours, your favourite chair, a window onto a silent beach. Now I am climbing the stairs—now I am opening the door—now I am inside (p. 194). Leave your worries at the foot of the stairs.

    7

    Disinhibit a little

    For most of us the cure is a long step in the opposite direction (p. 182). Do at least three of Maltz's five today (pp. 185–186).

      8

      Evening: that winning feeling

      Relive a past success in detail until you find yourself feeling just as you felt then, then picture tomorrow's task as already done (pp. 233–234).
